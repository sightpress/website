AI is what we make of it

By Pedro Ferreira

Artificial intelligence isn't a monster and it isn't a miracle. It's a tool. Used with care, it does real good. Used carelessly, it does real harm. Which of those we get depends on people, not on the machine.

This is an opinion piece, and I'm not neutral. I'm a blind developer, I use AI every working day, and I think the people who could gain most from it are too often told to keep away. I'll set out what AI does well, what it does badly, and the rules I think we should all use it by.

We have been here before

#Link to this section: We have been here before

Every important technology arrived with a wave of fear. People worried that the printing press would spread lies, that electricity would burn down cities, that the telephone would end real conversation and that the internet would rot our minds. Some of those fears were partly right. Printed lies did spread. The internet does have its dark corners.

But nobody sensible argues today that we should have stopped printing books or switched off the web. We learned, slowly and imperfectly, to use them well. We wrote laws, built habits and set expectations. The technology didn't decide the outcome. We did.

AI is the same. The question isn't whether it's good or bad. The question is how we choose to use it.

What AI does well

#Link to this section: What AI does well

The everyday benefit of AI isn't dramatic, and that's exactly why it matters. It takes the tiring, repetitive part out of ordinary work.

  • It finds things. A researcher can search a mountain of papers and find the five that matter.
  • It translates. A small charity with no budget for a translator can offer its pages in a second language.
  • It explains. A developer can ask what an unfamiliar library does instead of spending an afternoon on forums.
  • It summarises. A writer can get the gist of a long report and decide whether it's worth reading in full.
  • It describes. It can tell someone who can't see what's in a photo, a chart or a screen.

None of this replaces thinking. It removes friction, so people have more time and energy to think. That's what good tools have always done.

What AI does badly

#Link to this section: What AI does badly

I'd be a poor advocate if I only told you the good parts. AI has real weaknesses, and anyone who uses it should know them.

  • It's confidently wrong. AI can invent a fact, a quote or a source and present it in exactly the same calm tone as the truth. It can describe a photo of a cat as a dog. It doesn't know when it doesn't know.
  • It can be biased. It learns from what people have written, and it repeats their blind spots: about disability, about gender, about which languages and places matter.
  • It makes deception cheap. Spam, fake reviews, cloned voices and fake photos are now easy for anyone to produce, in bulk.
  • It can leak what you give it. Whatever you paste in leaves your hands. A document full of other people's personal details isn't yours to send to a stranger's server.
  • It raises fair questions about where it came from. Writers and artists have good reason to ask whether their work was used to build these tools without their permission, and the energy that large models use isn't free either.
  • It can wear down your own skills. If you stop checking its work, you slowly lose the ability to.
  • It concentrates power. A handful of companies own the largest models, and they decide what those models will and won't do.

These are serious problems. But look at them closely and most of them are problems of use: what we trust it with, what we check, what we send, and who we let make the rules. That's why I think the answer is to use AI well, not to refuse it.

It isn't nuclear, and it isn't genetics

#Link to this section: It isn't nuclear, and it isn't genetics

I often hear AI compared to nuclear weapons or genetic engineering. I think that comparison is wrong, and I think it does harm.

Splitting the atom gave us a weapon that can destroy a city in seconds. Editing the genes of living things can change them for good, and those changes can pass to every generation that follows. A single mistake with either can be permanent and enormous, and they deserve the strictest controls we have.

AI, as we actually use it today, is different in kind. It writes text, describes pictures, suggests code and answers questions. Its mistakes are real, but most of them are the kind a person can catch: a wrong fact, a clumsy sentence, a bug. We can check them, correct them, and switch the tool off.

That doesn't mean AI needs no rules. It needs good ones: honesty about what it is, respect for privacy, and people held responsible for what they publish and decide. But treating a writing assistant as if it were a warhead makes us afraid of the wrong things, and it pushes useful tools away from the people who need them most.

The fear of losing control

#Link to this section: The fear of losing control

The deepest fear about AI isn't about wrong facts. It's the fear that machines will start making our choices for us. But a tool has no will of its own. It only has the authority we hand to it, and we don't have to hand it any. Religious fears show this clearly. Many people of faith worry, in ways that deserve respect rather than mockery, that building a thinking machine is people trying to play God, or that we'll trust a machine's answer over our own conscience and treat it as a modern idol. Underneath those worries is a simple question: who is in charge of a human life? My answer is the same as theirs. People are. A machine can only become an idol if we choose to bow to it.

The business wars between the largest technology companies show the same thing from the other side. Some of the loudest warnings that AI might "take over" come from inside that race, and they're useful to the people making them: a frightening product sounds like a powerful one, and rules written in a panic tend to favour the few companies big enough to follow them. Meanwhile, the real loss of control looks far more ordinary: a feed designed to keep you scrolling, a recommendation built to make you buy, an "agent" that clicks "accept" on your behalf. That isn't a machine taking power. It's a company deciding how much of your choice to keep for itself. In both cases the answer is the same: keep the decision with the person.

What it means to a blind developer

#Link to this section: What it means to a blind developer

When I lost my sight, I lost the ability to glance. I can no longer look at a screenshot a colleague pastes into a chat, a chart in a report or a photo someone sends me. I can't skim a long page for the one paragraph I need. Everything reaches me one line at a time, through a screen reader, in the order someone else decided.

AI gives some of that back. It can describe an image that has no alt text. It can tell me what's on a screen that my screen reader announces as "Button. Button. Graphic." It can summarise a long document so I know where to start listening. It can explain an error buried in a wall of output, or describe a diagram that would otherwise be a blank space.

For a sighted person these are conveniences. For a blind person they're often the difference between doing the work and asking someone else to do it. Independence isn't a small thing.

Blind content creators gain in the same way. AI can suggest a first draft of alt text for a photo you took but can't see, check that your headings make sense, or turn your notes into a tidy article. You still decide what's true and what gets published. But you're no longer stuck at the first step.

The weaknesses matter more for us, too. A sighted person can glance at a photo and see that the AI got it wrong. I can't. That's exactly why I'm strict about how I use it.

How I use it, day to day

#Link to this section: How I use it, day to day

Opinions are cheap, so here's what AI actually does in my working week while I build SightPress.

Proofreading my writing. I can't see a typo. A screen reader reads "form" and "from" with equal confidence, and a missing word slips past the ear far more easily than the eye. So once I've written something, this article included, I ask an AI to proofread it: spelling, grammar, repeated words, sentences that run on, American spellings that have crept in. I ask it to keep my voice and to list every change, so I can listen to each one and accept or reject it. The ideas, the opinions and the final wording are mine. The AI is the second pair of eyes I no longer have.

Planning coding tasks. Before I write code for a new feature, I describe what I want and ask the AI to help me break it into steps: which parts of the system it touches, what could go wrong, what order to build things in, and what "done" looks like. A plan in plain text is far easier to read with a screen reader than dozens of open files. I argue with the plan, cut what I don't need and change what I disagree with before I start. Every stage of SightPress begins with a written plan like that, and the decisions in it are mine.

Writing automated tests. This is where AI helps me most. I can't glance at a page to see whether a change broke something, so I rely on tests to tell me, in words, what passed and what failed. I ask the AI to draft tests for the code I've written: the normal case, the edge cases, the errors, and checks that the admin panel's markup stays accessible. Then I read them and ask the awkward question: does this test check real behaviour, or does it just repeat the code back to itself? Unit tests must cover every line of SightPress's core code before a change is accepted, and AI makes that standard achievable for one person. The tests keep the AI honest too. When it suggests wrong code, a test usually catches it before I do.

The pattern is the same every time. The AI does the tiring, repetitive part. I review, decide and take responsibility.

The rules I use it by

#Link to this section: The rules I use it by

I don't think "be careful" is enough advice. Here's what I actually believe, and what I'd ask anyone using AI, in SightPress or anywhere else, to hold to.

  1. A person makes every decision. AI can suggest, draft and explain. It doesn't publish, send, buy or agree to anything on anyone's behalf. If a tool wants to act in your name, you should be able to see what it did and undo it.
  2. If you can't check it, don't publish it. Treat everything AI writes as a first draft from a confident stranger. Check facts against a real source. If you can't check something yourself, ask someone who can before it goes out.
  3. You're responsible, not the tool. "The AI wrote it" is never an excuse. Your name is on the page.
  4. Only share what's yours to share. Don't paste other people's personal details, private messages or confidential documents into an AI service unless you know where they go and you have the right to send them.
  5. Keep it optional and replaceable. Prefer tools you can switch off, swap for another provider or do without. No one should be forced to use AI to get a job done, and no one should be locked in to one company.
  6. Use it to do more, not to care less. AI should free your time for the thinking only you can do. If it's making you stop thinking, you're using it wrong.
  7. Don't use it to deceive. No fake reviews, no fake voices, no fake people. Be honest about where AI has helped when it matters to your readers.

And one more, aimed at the people who make the rules: refusing AI isn't automatically the safe or ethical choice. If a ban or a blanket "no AI" policy shuts blind and disabled people out of tools that give them independence, while everyone else carries on, that isn't caution. It's another barrier.

How SightPress puts this into practice

#Link to this section: How SightPress puts this into practice

These rules aren't just words in an article. They shape how the SightPress AI assistant works.

  • It's off unless you turn it on. New sites start without it. Every task in SightPress can be done without AI.
  • You choose the provider. An admin connects their own key from Anthropic, OpenAI or Mistral, or any service or self-hosted server that works like OpenAI's. Nothing is sent anywhere until they do. Keys are stored encrypted, and a monthly budget keeps costs under control.
  • It suggests; it never decides. Nothing the assistant writes is used until a person inserts it and saves. It never publishes anything on its own.
  • Unchecked AI work can't go live. AI-written alt text and translations stay marked until someone has checked them, and a page that uses them can't be published before then.

That's what using AI well looks like in practice: not blind trust and not blind fear, but a tool kept in human hands.

My view

#Link to this section: My view

I've lived through the whole history of the web, from the first static pages to today. I've seen every new technology called the end of something. Most of them turned out to be the beginning of something instead.

I'm not afraid of AI. I use it every day, and it has never made a decision for me, because I've never let it. What I am afraid of is the people who could gain most from it, blind people, disabled people and small organisations with no budget, being told it's too dangerous to touch while everyone else carries on using it.

AI won't save us and it won't destroy us. It will do what we ask of it, as well or as badly as we allow. So let's ask it to do good, check its work, and keep the decisions where they belong: with us.
